## Central adaptation finding

These pages support a **strategy game about relationships, promises, journeys, and the cost of preserving beauty** more strongly than a conventional conquer-the-map game. The fundamental tragedy is not weak military technique: gifted people make defensible desires absolute, then turn friends, objects, and whole landscapes into instruments of those desires. Good decisions still need material means; bad political commitments can make superior means useless.

The player's scale should be local in action and historical in consequence. The author explicitly foregrounds apparently small, unknown actors who achieve what armies cannot (letter, PDF 17). This also supports the requested image of tiny people in enormous landscapes: significance need not be expressed as physical size.

## Source themes with causal evidence

1. **Making is not ownership of everything that makes creation possible.** Aulë delights in making and giving rather than possession (30, 38). The Noldor initially give gems freely (79). Fëanor later hoards the Silmarils and forgets that their light was not his own (89–90). At the crisis, Yavanna needs their light to restore the Trees, while Fëanor sees breaking them as breaking his heart (101–102). Proposed mechanic: each great work has makers, materials, gifts, and obligations in its provenance. Holding it gives power but does not extinguish competing claims. Do not reduce Silmarils to high-value currency.

2. **Information becomes action through relationships.** Melkor's lies spread through trusted friends, acquire embellishments, and can feel like the hearer's own insight (89–91). Fëanor's actions then create the appearance that the lies were true: banishment and Finwë's departure leave Fingolfin ruling Tirion (92). Fingon's soldiers intervene at Alqualondë before understanding the quarrel (111). Proposed mechanic: intelligence records source, confidence, delay, and interested parties. A rumor can change recruitment and diplomacy even after its factual basis is disproved. This is a better foundation for dialogue-led strategy than an all-knowing diplomacy screen.

3. **A promise can destroy strategic flexibility.** Fëanor's oath includes any keeper of a Silmaril, good or evil, rather than Morgoth alone (106). Fingolfin's reconciliation promise also constrains his later choice (98, 107). The larger following does not recognize Fëanor as king but departs with him anyway (107). Proposed mechanic: keep an explicit commitment ledger with witnesses, beneficiaries, conflicting duties, and possible consequences. Do not represent all oaths as interchangeable timed buffs.

4. **Rescue and domination can share a vocabulary.** Melkor tells himself he will order the world for the Children while actually seeking mastery (29). The Valar's summons mixes care with desire for the Elves' companionship; Ulmo argues for their freedom to heal Middle-earth (66–67). The narrative directly warns that attempts by the Ainur to force the Children seldom prosper, even with good intentions (51). Proposed mechanic: protection has an autonomy cost. A safe settlement can become dependent, excluded, or estranged without the game declaring all protection evil.

5. **Distance creates cultures and unequal opportunities.** The journey divides peoples because some prefer the places they encounter, others fear barriers, and others wait for someone missing (67–69, 75–78). Long separation on Tol Eressëa changes the Teleri's speech (78). Proposed mechanic: refugees and communities retain destinations, loyalties, memories, and language needs; they are not fungible population units. Routes are political choices.

6. **Victory and emergency responses remake the map.** Destroying the Lamps permanently mars geography (46–47); defeating Utumno changes coastlines and still leaves hidden threats alive (65–66). Ungoliant grows through consuming light and through power ceded by Morgoth (98–99, 103). Proposed mechanic: exceptional force changes habitats and future routes. Avoid making divine devastation a cheaply reusable ultimate ability.

7. **Loss can become understanding or entitlement.** Nienna teaches pity and endurance and turns sorrow to wisdom (39); Fëanor's grief for his father exceeds his attachment to the jewels, yet his speech reproduces Morgoth's lies (102, 105). Nerdanel wants to understand minds instead of master them (85). Proposed inner voices may embody Memory, Pity, Craft, Pride, and Resolve, each revealing something and potentially overreaching. These are invented psychological systems, not literal canonical spirits occupying a character.

8. **A work's strategic utility does not erase its intimate meaning.** Olwë answers Fëanor's demand for ships by explaining their irreplaceable craft and collective labor (110–111). Fëanor subsequently burns the very ships he killed to obtain (113). Proposed mechanic: boats, archives, gardens, and tools can be transport/infrastructure and unique communal heritage simultaneously. Their destruction must remove more than a production number.

## Playable dilemmas grounded in this section

All the choices below are **adaptation proposals**. Some are local choices compatible with fixed history; changing named canonical outcomes creates an alternate history and should be labeled accordingly.

| Source situation | Playable dilemma | Strategic consequence |
|---|---|---|
| A messenger hears of missing travelers before Oromë's arrival (64–65) | Spread an urgent warning, investigate its source, or escort people while withholding certainty. | Public fear, exposure time, community trust, recognition of a real ally. |
| Summons westward (66–69) | Continue with the host, provision those who remain, or delay for the missing. | Safety and access to learning versus separation, autonomy, and loss of companions. |
| Rumor-driven arms race (90–92) | Reveal another house's armament, build your own, seek witnesses, or attempt a joint inquiry. | Deterrence, panic, prestige, and the risk that a protective action confirms a hostile story. |
| Flight under haste (106–110) | Carry food and tools, records and irreplaceable craft, or equipment for war. | Present survivability versus future capacity and what a community can remember or rebuild. |
| Alqualondë (110–111) | Refuse a command, evacuate noncombatants, preserve testimony, or enter a battle with incomplete information. | Immediate lives, complicity, command trust, future reconciliation. It should never be a cheerful tutorial rewarding massacre. |
| Prophecy of the North (111–112) | Turn back with Finarfin or accompany people who continue. | Returning is a viable moral and political decision, not a game-over for cowardice; the text explicitly excludes fear as the stayers' motive (107). |
| After loss of ships (113–114) | Allocate passage or crossing support, retain scouts, rescue stragglers, abandon cargo. | Trust, endurance, lost artworks, and later resentment. Canon fixes the betrayal and crossing; a retainer-scale scenario can vary who survives without pretending to rewrite them. |

The best bounded tutorial from this material is a council and migration episode, with a messenger, quartermaster, or household steward as the invented playable role. Playing an omnipotent Fëanor or a directly commanded Vala would make too much of the tragedy appear avoidable through simple optimization. If the main campaign begins later in Beleriand, this section works as inherited memory, a small number of playable recollections, and disputed testimony rather than hours of mandatory cosmic exposition.

## Character opportunities

- **Nerdanel (85, 87):** strong will, patience, understanding of others, and an eventual breach with Fëanor. A valuable model for a consequential counselor whose judgment is neither obedient nor infallible. Do not assign invented actions at Alqualondë to her as fact.
- **Fëanor (84–94, 101–113):** artist, inventor, bereaved son, rhetorician, and destructive claimant. Preserve his gifts and human motivations; neither a cartoon villain nor an uncomplicated freedom hero fits these pages.
- **Fingolfin (98, 107, 113–114):** distrusts a course yet follows to avoid abandoning his people, then leads through betrayal and deadly terrain. Strong focus for leadership under constrained choice.
- **Finarfin (106–112):** a persuasive case for restraint, kinship across political camps, repentance, and rebuilding. His return must retain dignity and agency.
- **Maedhros (113):** assumes the ships will return for Fingon and stands aside when ordered burned. A sharp visual and dialogue scene: a single figure refusing participation while still implicated by oath and family.
- **Olwë (110–111):** care, friendship, boundaries, and skilled labor, all expressed through the ships. The refusal is principled rather than petty obstruction.
- **Aulë/Yavanna (56–59):** craft and living systems both have legitimate claims. Their exchange is neither a simple anti-industry slogan nor permission for limitless extraction.
- **Ulmo/Nienna (37–41, 50):** remote care, pity, and endurance can influence play through water, songs, witnesses, and dreams without becoming quest dispensers standing on every road.

## Objects and environments

**Objects:** Silmarils as light-bearing works and contested obligations (89–90); swan-ships as labor, home, and transport (80, 110–113); Fëanor's distant-seeing crystals (85, named there descriptively rather than here asserting a later name); Rúmil's signs and Fëanor's letters as knowledge infrastructure (84–85); the lamps of Mindon and Alqualondë as orientation and memory (78, 80, 108); modest portable works of craft carried by the departing host as both consolation and burden (108). Most useful everyday inventory should consist of original supporting props, clearly proposed rather than falsely presented as named book artifacts.

**Environmental direction from text:** star-dim lakes and waterfall sound at Cuiviénen (63); gigantic woods and sky-piercing mountains along migration routes (68); pearl halls and a harbor through a natural arch at Alqualondë (80); Tirion's terraces, stairs, and lone lamp dissolving in sea fog after the Darkening (104); inaccessible black ravines below coastal mountains in Avathar (96–97); clashing ice, obscuring fog, and narrow lethal waterways on the Helcaraxë (113–114). The land should exhibit what happened there, not serve only as a tactical surface.

**Illustrations that directly corroborate the user's scale preference:** PDF 70 places small shoreline figures beneath a vast sky; 77 contrasts tiny shoreline travelers/boats with monumental dark cliffs; 115 shows a winding procession of tiny people disappearing across immense ice. These are observations of the supplied Ted Nasmith illustrations, not proposals to reuse or copy their exact compositions. Page 109 instead offers an intimate harbor scene useful for prop density and readable silhouettes. The report can recommend landscape-dominant travel framing plus portraits and inspection views for emotional detail, avoiding oversized map pawns merely to show faces.

**Proposed visual implementation targets:** terrain and atmosphere occupy roughly 80–90% of a normal exploration frame; figures around 12–28 pixels high at travel zoom and 35–60 pixels at local inspection zoom in a 1080p reference viewport; these numbers require prototype testing. Use silhouette, cloak accent, movement, selected-ground marker, and accessible labels for recognition. Keep interaction targets larger than the artwork. Preserve beautiful individual objects with closer inspection art, not constant magnification of the entire map. The book's light is narratively variable: starlight, intermingled silver/gold, abrupt deprivation, torchlight, then later celestial light. A uniformly grey palette would discard a major source theme.

## Canon and design traps

- Do not present Elves as immune to death or sorrow. They can be slain or waste in grief, remain bound to the world, and may eventually return from Mandos (51–52).
- Do not turn divine foreknowledge into a complete deterministic information interface: the Ainur's knowledge is incomplete; entering time makes events feel new even to them (28–31, 64).
- Do not describe the Valar as literally imprisoning all Elves in Aman. The text explicitly grants freedom to depart; Fëanor's own exile by oath is distinct (92, 107).
- Do not assert that Melkor taught Fëanor to make the Silmarils. His claim to that influence is explicitly a lie (87).
- Distinguish asserted event from attributed belief: the Orc-origin account is framed as what the wise hold, with acknowledged limits of knowledge (65); Ungoliant's origin and ultimate self-devouring are uncertain reports (96, 104).
- Do not make Men a lesser substitute for Elves: mortality includes a distinctive freedom and an unknown destiny, not merely a short lifespan penalty (51–52).
- Do not use the founding music as license to make everything subjective or morally equivalent. The narrative identifies coercion, treachery, and Kinslaying as wrong even while portraying complex motives.
- Avoid compressing all peoples, houses, Valar, and geography into the opening hour. Learn names through relationships and choices; the index exists because the book itself recognizes its naming burden (7–8).
- The proposed Disco Elysium connection should be psychological multiplicity, painterly atmosphere, political dialogue, and consequential failure. A direct transplant of modern cynicism, uniforms, politics, or comic voice would flatten this text's register and cosmology.

## Check of the proposed Brethil campaign premise

The proposed **local council/route strategy campaign in Brethil shortly after the fall of Tol Sirion, under delegated authority from Halmir with Beleg as a contact**, is well supported as an original story situated around canonical events. The campaign office, original officers, twelve-week duration, exact schedule, resources, and local choices are design inventions. The following checks used additional direct reading of PDF 179–184 and 195–198, with the refugee passage on 192–193 and the geography passage on 151 also checked.

**Chronology anchors:** Minas Tirith at Tol Sirion holds for nearly two years after the Dagor Bragollach and falls to Sauron after Fingolfin's death (195). Orodreth escapes to Nargothrond. The fall opens the western pass and Orcs come down Sirion. Halmir then sends swift word to Thingol; Beleg brings Sindar equipped with axes into Brethil, and their combined ambush destroys an Orc legion (196). The result is watchful peace in Brethil and many years in which Orcs do not cross the Teiglin, giving Nargothrond respite (196–197). The book does not specify twelve weeks for this sequence: label that as a chosen game scale, not a recovered chronology. A campaign preparing for and coping with this battle fits; routine large Orc invasions immediately afterward would conflict with the stated respite.

**Youth and autonomy:** Húrin and Huor are living with Haldir, their maternal uncle, as fosterlings. Both enter the battle; Huor is thirteen. They become separated, cross Brithiach into Dimbar under river mist, and are carried by Eagles to Gondolin (197). They are not adult military leaders available for the player to recruit here. Keeping this canonical disappearance at a distance lets the player respond to loss reports without choosing or revealing the Eagles' destination. Gondolin is hidden, and even the brothers do not know a traversable route to it (197–198).

**Political jurisdiction:** Brethil lies between Teiglin and Sirion, and outside the Girdle of Melian. Thingol claims it, but Finrod obtained permission for Haleth's people to dwell there freely, on condition that they guard the Crossings of Teiglin against enemies and keep Orcs from their woods (183). This supports disputed burdens and negotiated assistance; it does not support casually treating Brethil as a magically protected district within Doriath. Haladin settlement tradition includes separately governed homesteads, followed by chosen chieftainship under emergency pressure (182–183), giving a strong basis for a council of original household representatives under Halmir without claiming that the book specifies that exact constitution.

**Routes:** Teiglin and its crossings define the western/southern approach toward Talath Dirnen and the Nargothrond hinterland. Sirion bounds the east; Brithiach provides access toward Dimbar northeast of Brethil. The threat from Tol-in-Gaurhoth approaches from upstream/north, through the Sirion corridor. Amon Obel provides an interior landmark on the supplied map. Local camps, stores, footpaths, lookout sites, ferries, and travel times need to be explicitly authored rather than reverse-engineered as canon. Do not label an Halmir-era settlement **Ephel Brandir** without a source establishing the name at this date: Brandir belongs to a later generation (382), and the later narrative's name cannot simply be retrojected.

**Refugees:** Emeldir already leads surviving women and children from Dorthonion to Brethil after the Bragollach; some remain among the Haladin and others continue to Dor-lómin, including Rían and Morwen (192–193). Thus refugee reception and the relations between established and displaced households are particularly well grounded. Avoid asserting that the precise arrival of named refugees falls within the invented twelve-week campaign unless the chosen chronology explicitly justifies it.

**Information and suspicion:** The immediate aftermath of Tol Sirion's fall contains a powerful source-grounded dilemma: Morgoth sends deceivers and sometimes releases captives whose wills remain constrained; genuine escapees are consequently distrusted and abandoned (195). This can drive a humane investigation and asylum system. It must not mechanically imply that refugees are usually traitors or that rejecting them is optimal. Give testimony, independent evidence, corroboration, care, and protective procedures practical value, while accepting that certainty is incomplete.

**Scope limits for a canon-compatible campaign:** The local player may determine provisioning, warning networks, individual rescues, household relations, routes, and the quality of Halmir's preparations. The player should not command Beleg like a unit card, overthrow Thingol, capture Tol-in-Gaurhoth, casually discover Gondolin, or kill canonical people whose later lives are fixed. Winning locally can mean preserving a community and enabling the historical defense, with different survivors, obligations, and memories. Changing those anchors is a valid alternate-history mode only if clearly declared.
