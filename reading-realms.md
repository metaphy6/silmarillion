## Recommended playable frame: the forest and crossings of Brethil

**Proposal:** a regional narrative strategy game centered on a newly appointed, original march-warden of the People of Haleth, working under Halmir during the aftermath of the Battle of Sudden Flame and the loss of Tol Sirion. The player organizes households, scouts, provisions, sanctuary, river crossings, and agreements with neighboring powers. Halmir remains the established canonical lord. Haleth is a foundational historical figure, **not a living party member in this period**.

The book provides unusually solid foundations for this frame:

- **Political mandate:** Haleth's people may live freely in Brethil if they guard the Crossings of Teiglin and bar Orcs (p183). The arrangement is obtained through Finrod and granted by Thingol; freedom, obligation, and contested title coexist.
- **Settlement texture:** homesteads, dispersed people, chosen leadership, and a taste for woodland solitude appear on pp182–185. A map of inhabited clearings and paths is more faithful than an instant walled capital.
- **Human pressure:** Emeldir leads survivors of Dorthonion to Brethil; some remain and some continue to Dor-lómin (pp192–193). Sanctuary is an actual material and political problem, not an invented excuse to add a refugee system.
- **Enemy pressure:** the fall of Tol Sirion exposes the western route (p195); this is a condition the player receives, not a battle they must reenact exactly.
- **Agency with precedent:** Halmir requests aid from Thingol, Beleg brings a force, and their ambush stops the advance; Brethil's defense gives Nargothrond time to recover (pp196–197). Small places can materially change others' chances.
- **Intimate stakes:** visiting or fostered kin, family obligations, and different senses of time complicate the war (pp197–198). These are sources for personal stories, not a reason to send canonical children into repeatable combat missions.

**Suggested map boundary:** most of the playable terrain is Brethil between Teiglin and Sirion, with a few adjoining approach routes and a strategic view of neighboring powers. The Crossings of Teiglin and Brithiach are separate route features. Do not collapse them into one generic bridge. Interior settlements, smaller paths, households, and work sites would be clearly marked original additions, compatible with the book rather than claimed as named canon.

The default campaign should cover a limited local crisis, perhaps one year over twelve council turns, with travel and field missions between councils. The round count and time compression are design choices. Do not try to simulate the entire four-century siege in this same campaign. Ancient history arrives through testimony, inherited agreements, monuments, and interruptions from outside.

**Player goal:** keep a viable community and an open route through the crisis. Viability can include evacuation when the cost of holding a position becomes too great. People saved, promises honored or renegotiated, viable woodland, route access, and surviving relationships are outcomes, not just a single territorial score. Success must change who survives, what institutions endure, and whom the player can trust; it should not simply decorate an unavoidable loss screen.

## A systems loop with real strategic decisions

The following loop is proposed, not found as a ruleset in the book:

1. Receive conflicting reports at a council. Each report has a named source, location, time, and known uncertainty.
2. Walk the relevant ground or send a limited envoy/scout. This spends time and risks absence from another problem, but reveals useful evidence.
3. Negotiate commitments: households provide work, warriors, beds, boats, or grain in return for specific promises.
4. Allocate a small number of parties and materials to routes, shelter, stores, scouting, and defense. Orders can include fallback and casualty limits.
5. Resolve movement and confrontations. Terrain, warnings, supply, relationships, and standing orders determine options; enemy information is incomplete.
6. Revisit the place and the people. The cleared path, damaged home, exhausted scout, fulfilled promise, or missing neighbor persists in the landscape.

Start with **four concrete constraints**: provisions, available labor, defensive readiness, and secrecy of routes. Treat trust and obligations as relationship-specific records, not another universal spendable resource. The book supports several forms of social power that should not all collapse into a generic influence currency.

### Example decision chain: shelter at a crossing

An original group of survivors arrives while scouts report movement from the north. Two travelers escaped captivity. One household knows a traveler; another fears that the route to its hiding place will be exposed.

- A blanket refusal conserves immediate supplies but loses labor, testimony, and goodwill, and may leave actual people exposed.
- Immediate unrestricted entry saves time but may spread sensitive route knowledge before conflicting accounts have been checked.
- A temporary camp with food, humane interviews, an escort, and access limited to public routes requires guards and supplies, delaying a patrol elsewhere.
- A negotiated arrangement with an allied clearing spreads the burden but adds travel risk and a future obligation.

The source establishes both coerced agents and truly escaped captives who are unfairly rejected (p195). Therefore the game must not make all survivors secretly dangerous or turn suspicion into an invariably correct strategy. Suspicion, evidence, and humanity should all matter. Outcomes should depend on information, resources, and the actual situation; a secret fixed punishment for compassion would undermine the premise.

### Example decision chain: the forest's resources

Extra timber could provide shelter and strengthen a stockade, but a broad new road also exposes movement, breaks cover, and affects households that rely on the woods. Negotiating modest repair, using an older route, importing work from an ally, or abandoning an exposed outpost creates different commitments. The ecological conflict of newcomers cutting trees and hunting in Ossiriand is explicit (p179); applying a similar conflict inside Brethil is an **adaptation inference**, not a reported Brethil incident.

### Example decision chain: aid with conditions

A request to Beleg's border wardens can bring expert help, as the book demonstrates. The proposed gameplay cost is a specific locally negotiated commitment, not an invented canonical tax: shared scouting, protection for a route, public acknowledgment, or a future obligation. The player may accept, narrow, renegotiate, or decline. Avoid making every ally a cynical extortionist; sincere help and costly generosity are essential to this source.

## The right use of a Disco Elysium-inspired approach

Use the user's requested influence as a proposal for painterly art, expressive portraits, intimate observation, politically charged dialogue, and a protagonist with competing ways of interpreting evidence. Do not replace this book's worldview with universal cynicism. Its characters can be proud, manipulative, fearful, coercive, and culpable, but compassion, friendship, beauty, loyalty, and sacrifice are real forces, not disguises for private gain.

A council of internal perspectives could be original: **Kinship** notices abandonment, **Craft** notices workmanship and material weakness, **Memory** recalls old promises, **Foresight** imagines risk, and **Defiance** resists subordination. These labels and this implementation are invented. They can disagree and overreach; high ability should grant a perspective, not omniscience. Do not recast this mechanic as literal canonical voices of the Valar unless choosing a much stronger and clearly labeled departure.

Dialogue must change strategy: who will march, who will guide, which road is safe, whether a store is opened, how a death is remembered. Atmospheric prose without these consequences would produce an illustrated conversation game with a strategy veneer.

## Character, object, and environment direction

### Characters

Use an original local ensemble so choices are not trapped by already fixed biographies. Give each person a practical competence, a relationship, a place they depend on, a sincere desire, and a line they will not cross. Examples are a survivor organizing shelter, a household head worried about a depleted winter store, a scout loyal to the forest but hostile to a new road, and an envoy whose patience reflects a lifespan different from the protagonist's. These are proposed characters, not named figures in the book.

Canonical figures are strongest as limited presences:

- **Halmir:** regional authority who can authorize or question the player's decisions; his successful cooperation is attested on p196.
- **Beleg:** an ally with capabilities and commitments outside the player's control, not a recruitable weapon upgrade (p196).
- **Emeldir:** a forceful organizer of an armed evacuation, not a passive victim; any encounter must respect the itinerary given on pp192–193.
- **Haleth:** memory, precedent, and a burial mound rather than a living advisor. Her refusal of tutelage and the price of her journey give that memory tension (pp182–183).
- **Finrod:** the standard by which a promise can be judged, potentially through an envoy or recollection. If directly present, choose a date consistent with his later quest and death (pp209–217).

Maeglin is useful evidence that technical contribution does not settle character (pp169–174), but he is not a fit for casually visiting a Brethil tavern. Hidden realms should remain difficult to know and enter.

### Objects

Named objects in these chapters have social and narrative functions before they have combat statistics:

| Source object | Canonical function | Design lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Nimphelos (p118) | A pearl valued by the chief of Belegost within reciprocal work and exchange. | An object's worth depends on its receiver, provenance, and relationship, not just rarity. |
| Daeron's runes (p120) | An invention adopted enthusiastically by Dwarves; oral knowledge is later lost with destruction. | Recordkeeping, copying, and the survival of memory can be actions with long consequences. |
| Ring of Felagund/Barahir (pp190,207–209) | A visible token of an actual oath; it gains recognition and safe conduct. | A small object can authenticate a claim and unlock a relationship without functioning as a magic key to every institution. |
| Silver crown of Nargothrond (p210) | The handling of it makes continuing legitimacy and delegated rule visible. | A ritual gesture can change authority; objects can carry office. |
| Lúthien's cloak (pp213–217) | Self-made craft, concealment, sleep, escape, and rescue. | Clothing can embody agency and specialist action rather than a passive armor score. |
| Angrist (pp221,228) | Extraordinary craft succeeds at one extraction and breaks during an attempt to take more. | Objects can have decisive limits; escalation and excess deserve consequences. |
| Haleth's mound (p183) | Public memory rooted in a particular landscape. | A memorial can anchor legitimacy, mourning, and competing readings of the past. |

Use ordinary original objects for most play: a repaired boat, provisions tally, worn cloak, split timber marker, or household token. Let their histories create importance. Do not scatter every famous artifact across a loot table, duplicate unique jewels, or turn craft into disposable rarity colors.

### Vast landscape, tiny inhabitants

The user wants beautiful details that remain small relative to the map. The source strongly supports that direction: walls of mountains, long river courses, forest interiors, sheer gorges, wide plains, and immense engineered halls consistently exceed human scale (pp118–119,149–155). The illustrations at pp147,156,194,200,204,215,219,226 offer distinct examples of figures subordinated to land, sky, forest, or water; their visual compositions are supporting reference, not permission to reproduce the illustrations.

**Proposal:** a fixed isometric regional view with generous negative space and figures around 1.5–2.5% of the viewport height at the default zoom. This is a testable starting hypothesis, not a final specification. Portraits, temporary close examination, distinct silhouettes, restrained selection contours, and sound keep people intelligible. The default view should preserve the player's sense of being a small company in a large world. Interaction hit areas may be larger than sprites. Do not force target acquisition onto a ten-pixel figure or plaster persistent labels over every inhabitant.

Divide detail by scale: landscape masses and waterways read at a distance; settlement organization reads at middle zoom; animation, clothing, tools, inscriptions, and faces reward inspection. A monumental scene should still have a clear route and legible playable edge. The map must be spacious rather than merely full of slow walks.

Brethil can carry several painted environments without becoming a generic biome sampler: a rain-dark river crossing; a clearing of low homes beneath large tree canopies; a misted boundary near Sirion; a high burial mound with quiet grass; a burned approach where refugees arrive. Exact local layouts and seasonal states are proposed.

## Scope and adaptation cautions

1. **Choose the scale of agency.** The text says the Noldor cannot overthrow Angband unaided (pp136,188). A faithful local campaign can preserve that limit while giving substantial control over local outcomes. A sandbox that permits conquering Angband is a separate counterfactual mode and should be labeled as such.
2. **Do not predetermine every small tragedy.** Major external history may be fixed, but a saved family, secure route, surviving settlement, or repaired alliance must stay saved when the player's decisions justify it.
3. **Keep oaths distinct.** Finrod's promise leads to self-sacrifice; the Fëanorian oath fuels possessive violence. A single oath-is-always-corruption meter would misread the book (pp190,209–217).
4. **Preserve the specificity of geography.** Rivers, crossings, blocked passages, and hidden borders are the mechanics. No universally traversable hex sea or building at the gate of every hidden realm. In this period Minas Tirith is Finrod's tower on Tol Sirion, not the later Gondorian city (pp151,195).
5. **Avoid automatic moral identities by ancestry or appearance.** The text describes both faithful followers of Bór and faithless followers of Ulfang (p196). Diplomacy should track people, action, and allegiance. Do not derive betrayal probabilities from skin tone.
6. **Keep coercion visible as coercion.** Eöl, Thingol, Celegorm, and Curufin each constrain others in different ways. Lúthien is a decisive actor; Aredhel has desires and takes action. Do not reduce either to a transferable marriage reward (pp165–174,207–214).
7. **Protect wonder.** Most magic here is special, relational, artistic, or bound to exceptional beings. Routine cooldown spells and a research queue for werewolves would flatten that distinction.
8. **Scope the first prototype tightly.** One crossing, one clearing, one woodland route, six original speaking characters, three interlocking disputes, and one approaching incursion are enough to test the design. Prototype the coupling between dialogue and strategic consequences before building a full continent.

## Highest-value findings for the main report

The core adaptation opportunity is **holding a small community together inside a landscape and history much greater than it**. Brethil provides actual duties, actual negotiated freedom, actual refugees, and an actual successful alliance. The book's strategic depth is in geography, divided authority, secrecy, costly promises, and differences in time horizon. War is the pressure that reveals these systems, rather than the only activity that needs a system.

The visual requirement and the narrative requirement reinforce each other: tiny figures make land, memory, and institutions feel larger than any individual, while portraits and consequential conversations keep those same individuals worth protecting.
