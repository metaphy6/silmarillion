# THE FOREST AND THE OATH

## A strategy game from The Silmarillion

**Design analysis and visual direction | 29 September 2026**

**Recommendation:** a single-player, isometric narrative strategy game about sustaining a small homeland during the wars of Beleriand. Lead people, negotiate commitments, protect routes, and decide what can be preserved. Let tiny figures inhabit an immense, expressive, painted world.

Working title only; not an announced or licensed product.

This original concept plate studies composition and scale. It is an invented local scene, not a canonical map or a working game. Generated with the built-in image-generation tool; the prompt accompanies the image in `concept-prompt.txt`.

## 01 / The game I recommend

**Play an original march-warden of Brethil, entrusted by Halmir with the safety of several woodland settlements and their crossings after the loss of Tol Sirion.** You have limited people, food, time, and authority. Neighbours can help, but they have their own obligations. A successful campaign leaves a community capable of living without you.

This setting directly connects the desired visual scale to the player's role. You can care deeply about a tiny person crossing a huge river because you know what they carry, who is waiting for them, and what your last decision cost them.

| Decision | Recommended starting point |
|---|---|
| Genre | Turn-based regional strategy with character-led councils and brief field visits |
| View | Fixed isometric composition; pan and zoom; landscape dominates |
| Player authority | Delegated local responsibility; Halmir remains the canonical lord |
| Main verbs | Observe, provision, negotiate, promise, assign, escort, conceal, defend, withdraw |
| Main campaign target | 12 weekly turns over one invented local crisis; roughly 4-6 hours, to test |
| Combat | Small-company encounters resolved through plans, terrain, warning and morale |
| Emotional centre | The tension between keeping a promise and keeping people alive |
| Visual centre | Beautiful, vulnerable human activity within enormous woods, rivers and old places |

**Why Brethil:** its people hold negotiated freedom with a duty to guard the Crossings of Teiglin; refugees actually arrive there; Halmir and Beleg actually cooperate effectively. There is room for local invention and meaningful success within the published history. [B: 182-183, 192-197]

The proposed player, office, weekly timetable, settlements, incidents and rules are additions. This report does not claim that the book describes this campaign.

**First playable test:** one crossing, two inhabited clearings, an alternative woodland route, six original speaking characters, three field parties and eight turns. Test whether a conversation changes the next map decision before expanding the geography.

## 02 / What the whole book contributes

The strongest adaptation principle is that **power changes relationships before it changes borders**. The major disasters repeatedly arise from possession, bad counsel, pride, damaged trust, and an inability to relinquish something beloved. Military force matters, but it rarely explains the whole outcome.

**Creation and stewardship.** The *Ainulindalë* makes the world an interdependent work whose makers understand only part of it. Aulë and Yavanna make the claims of craft and living things concrete. A strategy system can therefore ask what construction enables, whom it serves, and what it damages. This is an interpretation for design, not a canonical ecology ruleset. [B: 26-33, 56-59]

**The First Age.** The jewels are irreplaceable works, inherited claims, and concentrated memory. The oath converts desire into an obligation that outlives its originator. The realms are politically divided and geographically specific; apparent security can conceal strategic weakness. Heroes rescue and preserve as often as they conquer. [B: 89-114, 135-163, 201-325]

**Númenor.** Fear of death becomes a political vulnerability. Sauron loses the military confrontation yet gains access to the court and progressively captures its judgment. Material growth continues while the society becomes crueler and less secure. A game should distinguish prosperity, legitimacy, safety, and freedom: a rising production total cannot represent all four. [B: 327-349]

**The Rings and the Third Age.** The desire to preserve beauty makes dependency attractive. The Three remain bound to the One despite Sauron's never having touched them. Ending the enemy's power also ends a form of preservation. This gives the design a powerful late-game question: which protections must you relinquish for a future to exist? [B: 360-362, 370-378]

The later works inform the design's systems and possible future campaigns. Their characters, technologies and artifacts do not migrate into First Age Brethil. Gandalf, Númenórean fleets and Rings of Power have no place in this opening campaign.

**Preserve genuine hope.** Pity, friendship, skilled work, rescue, and voluntary sacrifice are effective forces in this book. Constant betrayal would be as reductive as constant military triumph.

## 03 / Turn the themes into decisions

| Source pattern | Proposed playable system | What the player must weigh |
|---|---|---|
| Fëanor's oath expands the field of enemies; Finrod's promise instead becomes costly loyalty. [106, 209-217, 319-324] | A commitment ledger recording beneficiary, witnesses, terms, deadline and conflict | Whether to fulfil, renegotiate or break a particular obligation; no universal oath-corruption meter |
| Melkor's rumours pass through trusted people; captives can be coerced or unjustly suspected. [89-92, 195] | Reports with named sources, age, corroboration and uncertainty | Time spent checking an account versus time lost responding |
| Hidden cities survive through secrecy but become attached to their isolation. [158-160, 303-307] | Routes have both usefulness and exposure | Opening a road improves movement while revealing where people live |
| The Dragon-helm draws followers and reveals Túrin's identity. [260-261] | Public symbols change recruitment and enemy knowledge | Whether prestige is worth visibility |
| A bridge improves access to Nargothrond and later admits disaster. [268, 270-272] | Infrastructure changes the route network for both sides | Immediate capacity versus the cost of defending or dismantling it |
| Idril prepares an escape route despite complacency. [306-309] | Reserve plans, shelters and withdrawal orders | Investment in an option that may look wasteful until it saves lives |
| Isildur rescues a fruit of Nimloth before the tree is destroyed. [341] | Living heritage that can be carried and replanted | Preserving a future rather than keeping every present possession |
| The Rings preserve while binding their users to another power. [361, 370-378] | Support agreements with visible dependencies | Whether to retain a benefit that compromises future independence |

All rules in this table are proposals. The examples are evidence for their dramatic value, not instructions to reproduce every event in one campaign.

**Make consequences spatial.** A broken promise closes a ford to your messengers. A negotiated welcome creates a new inhabited clearing. A damaged alliance removes a guide, making a route longer. A rescued craftsperson repairs a boat. Players should be able to see the political story in the map.

**Keep consequences explainable.** After resolution, show the observed cause, remaining uncertainty and new choices. Avoid a hidden morality score that declares the player's ending without revealing how it was earned.

## 04 / Where to set the game

These are design judgments, not audience research or commercial demand evidence.

| Campaign | Strength | Principal difficulty | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brethil after Tol Sirion's fall | Negotiated autonomy, refugees, forest geography, limited forces and successful cooperation | Much everyday life must be invented carefully | Best first campaign |
| Havens of Sirion late in the First Age | Displaced communities, shipbuilding, an intensely contested heirloom, approaching disaster | Major outcomes constrain the player; Elwing must retain agency | Strong later campaign about sanctuary and departure |
| Gondolin before its fall | Monumental architecture, secrecy, competing counsel, engineering and evacuation | A city-scale production burden; foreknowledge can trivialise decisions | Strong focused expansion once the core systems work |
| Númenor under mounting court influence | Political persuasion, prosperity, complicity, mortality and naval logistics | A different map, institutions, timescale and cast | Best alternative if court politics is the priority |
| All Beleriand across the First Age | Maximum historical scope and many factions | Generations, fixed biographies, huge writing/art load, competing game scales | Defer; too broad to validate this concept |

**Recommended temporal boundary:** an invented twelve-week interval around Halmir's response to the fall of Tol Sirion, while Sauron still holds the island and before Lúthien liberates it. Do not compress all the remaining First Age disasters into those weeks. Halmir and Beleg's defense is the historical anchor; the player's local contribution is invented. Their victory begins years of relative security, so a faithful continuation cannot immediately reset Brethil to endless identical invasions. [B: 195-197, 217]

**Recommended geographic boundary:** a playable part of Brethil, with Teiglin on its western/southern side and Sirion on its eastern side. Brithiach and the Crossings of Teiglin are different places on different rivers. Tol Sirion lies upstream and remains an off-map source of danger. [B: 149-155, 394-395, 442-446]

A regional overview can show the surrounding powers and routes without pretending that a single walking map spans the continent. Local maps contain playable clearings, trails, slopes and fords; the overview contains travel commitments and reports.

The intended audience is a hypothesis: players who enjoy planning under pressure, strong writing and atmosphere, and who tolerate deliberation between actions. The first test must include both Tolkien readers and players unfamiliar with the names.

## 05 / The strategic loop

**One turn is one week.** Time advances only when the player commits orders. Looking around, reading and comparing plans are free; a field investigation uses a party's weekly assignment. An individual character cannot attend a distant council and lead an expedition in the same turn.

1. **Hear reports.** See the location, messenger, age and confidence of each claim. A route can be unexplored, recently observed or known to be threatened.
2. **Inspect or investigate.** Examine known ground now, or plan a scout assignment into unknown ground. Dispatched scouts report after the week's resolution, informing the next council; their future findings cannot improve this week's other orders retroactively.
3. **Convene a council.** Negotiate shelter, work, escorts and access. People may consent, refuse or offer alternatives.
4. **Issue orders.** Assign parties, provisions and materials. Choose routes, priorities, acceptable risk and fallback conditions.
5. **Commit the week.** Resolve movement, work, contact and encounters. Critical new situations can pause resolution for a bounded decision.
6. **Return to the consequences.** Changed places, injuries, arrivals, testimony and obligations become next week's situation.

| State | Representation | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| People and labour | Named officers plus household groups; each party gets one assignment per week | Every deployment has an opportunity cost |
| Provisions | Stocks at locations, transport capacity and consumption | Supply routes matter; no global warehouse |
| Materials | A small pool of practical work supplies | Repair, shelter and defense compete without a huge crafting tree |
| Readiness | Fatigue, wounds, cohesion and equipment by party | Repeated success cannot erase exhaustion |
| Relationships | Specific trust, grievances, access and promises | Cooperation has a history; trust is not a spendable coin |
| Exposure | What an adversary plausibly knows about routes and settlements | Secrecy emerges from actions and observation |

**Success:** sustain a viable local community and a workable route network while meeting or honestly revising its commitments. Outcomes can include a defended homeland, a negotiated smaller refuge, or a successful local relocation. Their value depends on people, autonomy, remaining stores and relationships, not hectares controlled.

**Failure:** the community loses the capacity to sustain or relocate itself. Local losses should normally open difficult recovery choices. Do not force every viable campaign to end in annihilation because the wider legend contains tragedy.

## 06 / A worked turn: The Far Bank

This is a wholly invented scenario for testing the rules, not a scene or quotation from Tolkien. All quantities are provisional balance values.

**Week 3.** Six resident household groups consume six provision units per week. The accessible local store holds 22 units; ordinary household work brings four more, independently of your deployable parties. Three exhausted refugee groups reach the far bank. Feeding them requires three more units weekly. A previous patrol saw tracks on the northern approach but could not identify their age. A storeholder offers six additional units, outside the 22 and deliverable locally this week, if you promise to protect her outlying clearing next week.

You have three parties: a scout party, a work party, and a flexible escort party. Escorting the refugees and preparing humane temporary shelter take the work and escort assignments. Northern reconnaissance takes the scout assignment. Your routine western patrol will therefore be absent.

| Plan | End-of-week provisions | Commitment and next problem |
|---|---:|---|
| Admit and provision all three groups; investigate north | 22 + 4 - 9 = 17 | Saves the arrivals; leaves the western path unwatched |
| Same plan, accepting the storeholder's offer | 22 + 4 + 6 - 9 = 23 | Gains time, but reserves an escort commitment next week |
| Guide the arrivals to a consenting allied clearing | 22 + 4 - 6 - 3 travel rations = 17 | Uses an escort and requires an actual prior agreement; capacity and welcome are checked |
| Refuse entry and assistance | 22 + 4 - 6 = 20 | Conserves supplies; people remain exposed and their future is tracked |

The first plan leaves a weekly deficit of five at unchanged collection. Refugees become potential workers after rest; the player must still decide whether to improve provisioning, share burdens or arrange onward travel. This arithmetic makes compassion a planning challenge without defining it as failure.

**Counsel scene, original writing:**

*The storeholder:* “I will open the store. Tell me whose watch stands outside it when the next cart comes.”

*Memory:* “She has asked you once before.”

*Mercy:* “Those on the bank have not eaten today.”

*Craft:* “The smaller carts can cross. The heavy one cannot.”

The voices reveal competing considerations; none selects the answer. Interview evidence may change a route assessment or reveal a needed skill. It must not secretly guarantee that kindness triggers betrayal. The source explicitly includes innocent escapees harmed by suspicion. [B: 195]

## 07 / War, uncertainty and lasting choices

**Combat should test the plan already made.** A forest defense asks where to watch, how to delay an advance, when to conceal movement, and which route remains open for withdrawal. At this scale, supply and local knowledge matter as much as a weapon rating.

Use a small-company order model: **watch, escort, screen, ambush, hold, withdraw**. Display known terrain and readiness modifiers before commitment. Show confidence bands when the enemy's strength is uncertain. Do not reveal exact hidden unit counts behind a mysterious percentage.

A confrontation can resolve on the existing local map in several phases: contact, response, withdrawal or pursuit, then rescue and recovery. The player changes a limited number of orders when new evidence appears. Elaborate individual action-point combat would become a second full game; defer it until the strategic loop has proven insufficient.

**Retreat must work.** A rear guard, a prepared ford and an escort destination create a successful withdrawal. Injuries, abandoned materials and broken contact are meaningful costs. Slaughter should not be the only significant combat outcome.

**No consequence-free delay.** Repeated scouting improves knowledge while consuming time and readiness; food is still consumed, arrivals still need shelter, and observed enemies may move. Conversely, opening a menu or reading a conversation never advances time.

**Separate uncertainty from arbitrariness.** A report can be incomplete or wrong, but its source and age are visible. Outcomes follow the world's state and the issued orders. A fixed seed and event log support debugging and consistent reloads; difficulty changes information or resources, not secret punishments for particular moral choices.

The *Nirnaeth Arnoediad* supplies the clearest source lesson: a coalition can lose through withheld participation, delayed intelligence, provocation and betrayal. Individual acts of defense still matter, especially when they preserve a retreat. [B: 237-244]

Legendary enemies remain exceptional. A dragon is a landscape-changing emergency requiring evacuation, alliance or an extraordinary story, rather than a repeatable source of crafting materials. Ordinary campaign pressure comes from patrols, raids, exposure and the growing inability to cover every route.

## 08 / Characters worth looking after

Small figures require strong identity elsewhere: a distinctive silhouette, a useful skill, a place in the community, a contradiction, and a relationship the player can change. The following six roles are original proposals; their titles are temporary design labels, not invented Elvish names.

| Character | Competence and desire | Complication | Map silhouette |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Warden, player | Organises routes and commitments; wants to justify entrusted authority | Can become controlling while believing every decision protects others | Weathered short mantle, walking staff, small pale shoulder patch |
| The Storekeeper | Knows supplies and households; wants fair contribution | Remembers emergencies in which her generosity was treated as an unlimited resource | Ochre wrap, keys and narrow tally board |
| The Scout | Reads weather, tracks and cover; wants the forest's independence | Shares crucial knowledge reluctantly when outsiders propose a road | Grey-green hood, low profile, long unstrung bow |
| The Boatwright | Repairs boats and crossings; wants good work to endure | A technically excellent crossing can also expose a refuge | Rolled sleeves, blue apron, carried tool roll |
| The Refugee Organiser | Keeps families together; wants a future beyond being a guest | Refuses to let gratitude become permanent political submission | Repaired russet cloak, bundles, decisive leading gesture |
| The Boundary Envoy | Connects local wardens to Doriath; wants reliable cooperation | Underestimates how costly delay feels to people with short lives | Slender silver-grey outline, restrained leaf-shaped fastening |

Let them disagree about methods without assigning each one a single permanent ideology. Give them private generosity, practical irritation and humour. A polished portrait cannot compensate for dialogue that only repeats a resource tooltip.

**Canonical presences:** Halmir grants and judges the mandate; Beleg is an independent ally; Emeldir's evacuation informs testimony and the refugee community. Any direct meeting with her needs a deliberate placement in the chronology. Haleth is remembered through precedent and her mound, not alive in this period. [B: 182-183, 192-197]

**Character art:** angular, expressive painted portraits; material wear; unusual but coherent costume shapes; selective warm accents. Show age, attention and fatigue rather than giving every person royal armour. Faces receive detail in portraits and conversations; map bodies retain believable small proportions.

## 09 / Tiny people, enormous world

**Make the environment large by composition, continuity and traversal choices.** A zoomed-out scene containing an oversized hero beside an empty background will not deliver the intended feeling. Trees, river systems, slopes and paths must set the visual rhythm; human activity occupies small, intentional pockets.

Use a fixed orthographic camera. A starting angle is 45 degrees around the vertical axis and approximately 35 degrees down from horizontal. This is a compositional baseline to test, not a requirement to display a diamond grid. Fixed rotation lets artists control overlap, light and the path of the eye.

| View at a 1920 × 1080 reference viewport | Starting scale target | Information shown |
|---|---|---|
| Regional overview | Parties aggregate to symbols when bodies become too small | Routes, delays, access and areas of uncertainty |
| Default playable view | Human figures about 18-28 px high, or 1.7-2.6% of viewport height | Distinct small groups, paths, camp activity, directional movement |
| Optional inspection zoom | Figures about 45-70 px high | Clothing shape, work gestures and local objects |
| Conversation / object inspection | Separate portrait or detail illustration | Face, emotional expression, craft and provenance |

These are proposed targets requiring actual playtests. The concept image shows the intended relationship but does not verify exact runtime sizes.

**Readability tools:** readable silhouettes; two or three value groups per costume; a small accent that survives zooming; a discreet selected-ground marker; optional outlines and names; keyboard cycling and a location roster. Selection regions can exceed the visible artwork. Never require someone to click an eight-pixel hand.

**Occlusion:** fade intervening tree canopies locally, reveal a character's ground position, and keep critical paths distinguishable from decoration. Do not solve the problem by enlarging every person or removing the forest.

Keep roughly 85-90% of the default frame visually devoted to the world, with compact controls at its edges. Long conversations may temporarily use a larger side panel. Text size and interface scaling remain independent of map zoom.

The supplied illustrations already support this instinct: PDF 115 places a small procession within enormous ice; 147 and 194 subordinate inhabitants to terrain. Borrow that scale relationship while developing an original painterly language.

## 10 / Paint, places and objects

**Art direction:** dark but chromatically alive. Rain-blue stone, deep viridian woods, silver birch, old ochre cloth, amber shelter light and sparse vermilion. Use broad painted masses at distance and concentrated detail around decisions. Preserve visible brushwork and incomplete edges; avoid uniformly sharp texture, glossy surfaces and a constant brown wash.

**Light has meaning.** A camp's warm light can imply welcome and exposure at the same time. Moonlit paths reveal another route through a familiar place. The loss of light should be noticeable because the world has previously contained beauty. The opening cosmology and the Trees make light too important to reduce to a generic dark-fantasy filter. [B: 48-52, 96-102, 124-127]

| Place | Visual identity | Strategic use |
|---|---|---|
| Teiglin crossing | Long dark water, exposed stones, tiny queues, low mist | Capacity, delays, escort and watched approaches |
| Inhabited forest clearing | Low roofs or shelters under high trees; work marks and stored bundles | Shelter, labour, local consent and provisions |
| Haleth's mound | Grass, a quiet rise and a clear margin in the trees | Memory, legitimacy and a council about inherited duties |
| Northern approach | Broken cover, ash carried by rain, tracks that cross older tracks | Report reliability, warning and defense in depth |
| Sirion boundary | Reeds, broad water and restricted passages | Diplomacy and access, distinct from the Teiglin crossing |

Except for named geography and Haleth's mound, these local compositions are proposed art and level design.

**Objects should have biographies.** A repaired boat can belong to a household, transport food, and carry people away from a lost home. An inherited clasp may identify an obligation. A letter can be copied, delayed, entrusted or withheld. Inspecting an item should answer who made it, who needs it and what a decision about it changes.

The book's named examples reinforce this: the ring of Barahir authenticates a promise; Angrist enables a singular act and breaks under excess; the Nauglamír becomes a dispute over labour, possession and authority; Nimloth's fruit carries continuity through destruction. [B: 207-209, 228, 292-296, 341]

Keep unique legendary objects out of ordinary loot generation. Most usable props should be modest original things with specific local histories. Save detailed object paintings for objects that actually change a decision.

## 11 / What to take from Disco Elysium

The useful connection is expressive observation: the same person or place can invite competing interpretations, and those interpretations can change what the player attempts. The official Psyche descriptions explicitly give faculties biases and unreliable suggestions. The official Thought Cabinet description connects internalised ideas to dialogue, events and mechanical effects. [D1, D2]

For this game, propose five **inner perspectives**: Memory, Mercy, Craft, Vigilance and Pride. They belong to the original protagonist's psychology. They are not the Valar speaking and do not certify supernatural truth.

| Perspective | What it notices | How it can mislead |
|---|---|---|
| Memory | Precedent, debts, altered testimony | Treats a present person as a repetition of an old betrayal |
| Mercy | Suffering and possibilities for repair | Underestimates the strain imposed on other vulnerable people |
| Craft | Materials, workmanship and practical alternatives | Assumes a social conflict can be repaired like a mechanism |
| Vigilance | Exposure, missing details and contingency | Confuses lack of certainty with evidence of danger |
| Pride | Dignity, manipulation and unacceptable terms | Makes retreat or apology feel like annihilation |

These particular perspectives and effects are original design proposals. A strong perspective opens observations and arguments; it does not automatically make them right.

**Beliefs become standing commitments.** After repeated decisions, the player can adopt a policy such as keeping a refuge open or refusing unwitnessed bargains. Adoption changes default orders and expectations; revising it requires explanation and creates political consequences. This adapts the general idea of thoughts with mechanical force into governance. Do not reproduce Disco's named thoughts, portraits or interface.

**Tone:** intimate, perceptive and occasionally funny, with room for exhaustion and wounded dignity. Tolkien's world also needs sincerity, wonder and a credible good. Modern detective slang, borrowed political labels and reflexive cynicism would work against the material.

Keep most routine exchanges short. Reserve substantial conversations for disputed evidence, irreversible commitments and changed relationships. Essential facts must appear in the order preview and journal even if the player skips atmospheric text. No key strategic rule should be buried inside an ambiguous poetic aside.

## 12 / Agency within the legend

The recommended mode is **a local story consistent with the published history**. The player does not determine whether the Two Trees fall, whether Fëanor's oath exists, or whether Angband can be conquered by an ordinary local army. They determine who survives this crisis, which routes remain usable, what trust is repaired, and what form the community takes.

Use three evidence labels during development:

- **Source fact:** an event, identity or relationship established in the supplied volume, with a PDF page reference.
- **Interpretation:** a design reading of what makes that fact significant.
- **Invention:** an added character, conversation, settlement, mechanic or possible local outcome.

Do not make a legend-compatible campaign a disguised sequence of losses. Brethil has a source precedent for real local success. Preserve earned outcomes for the duration of the campaign. A later historical catastrophe need not retroactively invalidate the people or institutions sustained in this story. [B: 196-197]

**Knowledge also has limits.** The player may know the book, but their warden receives reports. Avoid fake mystery about famous events; build tension around costs, timing and the fate of original people instead. Hidden Gondolin is not an ordinary selectable shopping destination.

**Authority has limits.** Halmir, Beleg, households and allied powers can refuse. Refusal should be intelligible from promises, responsibilities and events. A recruit button should not turn every named hero into an obedient party member.

**Keep the source's uncertainty.** Its foreword acknowledges a composite text. Some lore is attributed belief; some outcomes are unknown. Amandil's ultimate fate, for example, is not established as a successful second Eärendil mission. The explanatory Waldman letter also reflects an earlier state of the mythology; its notes explicitly correct the old numbering of Númenor's kings. [B: 6-8, 344, 458]

If a later game offers alternate history, label the divergence and let major events truly change. Mixing fixed outcomes with promises of unrestricted freedom would be misleading. That larger mode requires additional writing and simulation and is outside the first prototype.

## 13 / How to build a convincing first slice

**Validate three things in order: the decision, the visual scale, and their combination.** Do not commission a continent before proving that a changed relationship produces an interesting route decision.

| Gate | Deliverable | What would justify proceeding |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Rules test | Paper or simple digital map; eight turns; three parties; food, routes and commitments | Players understand why they cannot do everything and try more than one viable plan |
| 2. Visual test | One painted crossing, six silhouettes, a portrait pair, zoom/selection/occlusion | Small figures remain findable; participants describe the world as vast rather than empty |
| 3. Connected slice | Councils, field investigation, orders, resolution, consequence scene, save/load | Dialogue changes a plan; the changed plan alters a place or relationship |
| 4. Production decision | Observed writing/art throughput, performance measurements, revised scope | The team can estimate a full campaign from completed work rather than illustration counts |

**Slice budget:** three connected local areas; six original speaking roles; three usable parties; three disputes sharing resources; one incursion with hold and withdrawal outcomes; one surviving-state epilogue. Use text and a small original soundscape first. Full voice acting, multiplayer, generational simulation, a full continent and a second tactical game are deferred.

**Suggested technical path:** for a desktop-first prototype, test Godot with a fixed orthographic Camera3D, simple terrain/occlusion geometry, painted surfaces and modest character models. The official camera supports orthographic projection; the quality of the painterly result still depends on art direction and rendering work. [G1]

If the first deliverable must run in a browser, a 2D Phaser scene with layered painted backgrounds and sprites is an alternative prototype path. [G2] No platform was specified, so browser deployment is not a requirement and no engine is finally selected here.

Keep the turn simulation independent of the renderer. Save parties, stocks, route state, commitments and the event log. Author dialogue against those shared variables. Chunk the map, limit active detail to visible areas, and measure zoomed-out performance; a single enormous texture is not a plan for a vast world.

No game has been implemented or playtested as part of this report. The included image is concept art only.

## 14 / What could make this fail

| Risk | Early evidence | Response |
|---|---|---|
| The game is mostly conversation | Players cannot name a map decision changed by dialogue | Make each major conversation alter access, information, labour or a commitment |
| It becomes generic survival management | Players remember only food totals | Attach costs to named people, actual places and previous promises |
| Tiny figures become frustrating | Frequent missed selections or lost parties | Larger hit regions, roster selection, outlines, occlusion handling and optional zoom |
| Vastness becomes waiting | Players spend time crossing known empty ground | Dispatch through the route map; let field scenes begin at the meaningful location |
| Tragedy makes decisions feel pointless | Players assume everyone dies regardless | Demonstrate a lasting rescue and a lasting alliance in the first three turns |
| One cautious plan dominates | Every test group waits and scouts indefinitely | Give observation real time/labour costs and offer productive, evidence-based risks |
| Writing/art scope grows uncontrollably | New places require unrelated assets and branching stories | Revisit locations in changed states; build events from persistent shared systems |

**Proposed acceptance checks:** in an initial six-person test, at least five participants can identify a party's next destination and select it without help; at least four can explain two consequences of a commitment; at least two viable plans emerge across repeated runs. These are iteration gates, not statistical proof of demand. Verify colour-independent route/status cues, scalable text and reduced-motion options.

For emotional validation, ask which person they worried about and why. For strategy validation, ask which order they changed after hearing new evidence. For visual validation, test a silent first impression before providing the intended art description.

**Production dependency:** a public direct adaptation needs its rights position resolved before a commercial plan is built around it. The Tolkien Estate's published FAQ restricts adaptation permissions; this report does not establish a licence or a clearance route. If rights are unavailable, preserve the general design principles in a substantially original world rather than merely renaming Tolkien's people and places. [R1]

Team size, platform, budget and production schedule are unknown. The prototype is the next basis for estimating cost. No revenue, demand or delivery-date claim is supported by this reading exercise.
